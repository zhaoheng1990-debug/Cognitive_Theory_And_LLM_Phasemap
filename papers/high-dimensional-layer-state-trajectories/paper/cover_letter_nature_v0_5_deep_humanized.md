We submit the Article, "Ordered layer-state trajectories enable analysis of transformer inference", for consideration in *Nature*. Mechanistic studies can localize information to parameters, heads, neurons and circuits. Those implementation components do not specify how a prompt-conditioned computation unfolds across depth. We follow the ordered sequence of high-dimensional layer states as a shared empirical object for observing, comparing and perturbing inference.

Across representative Qwen, Llama and Gemma checkpoints, direct hidden states provided stronger mechanism coordinates than vocabulary readbacks, and realized layer order was 1.60-2.24 times more chord-aligned than matched endpoint-preserving shuffles. The geometric signature recurred in controlled lexical, arithmetic and multiple-choice tasks. Random initializations showed stronger alignment, while reordering trained blocks changed outputs. Component-swap controls then separated an architecture-compatible propagation scaffold from training-organized task coordinates and functionally consequential execution.

We used a confidence-gated local-transition actuator to test a declared output boundary. It switched the full-vocabulary top token from the provenance-defined conflict candidate to the clean candidate in 17/87, 13/87 and 9/87 held-out relation-graph closure prompts in Qwen, Llama and Gemma, with 0/203, 2/203 and 0/203 non-target output changes. Failed additive interventions, the absence of corrected direction specificity and non-confirmatory mixed-arithmetic transfer are reported alongside this result. The claim is limited to a declared candidate boundary in this task family. It does not cover answer correctness, deployment control or a universal intervention direction.

For a broad readership, the Article supplies a testable process-level design. In distributed systems with measurable state trajectories and legitimate interventions, it asks whether a chosen process object supports informative coordinates, order-sensitive nulls and selective perturbations. This is a methodological transfer question. It does not claim that artificial, physical and biological systems share a mechanism.
